# Relay NW — ICP Deep Dive

**Date:** May 17, 2026
**Question:** Who is the absolute best customer for Relay NW today?

## The recommendation in one sentence

**Solo-to-two-truck residential trades operators in the Pacific Northwest, $150K–$500K annual revenue, who currently lose calls during jobs and either pay nothing or pay too much for a clunky answering service.** Specifically: independent plumbers, garage-door techs, junk removal, and mobile mechanics — owner-operators where the buyer, user, and decision-maker are the same person.

This is **not** "plumbers." Plumbers is too broad and too crowded. The slice is "owner-operators too small for ServiceTitan/LeadTruffle/Intry and too price-sensitive to pay $229/mo for an AI receptionist they don't trust."

## The persona

**Marcus, 41. Owns Marcus Plumbing in Vancouver, WA.** Started his own shop three years ago after a decade with a bigger company. One van (his own). $280K trailing-12 revenue. Spends $900/mo on Google LSA, $99/mo on HouseCall Pro, $60/mo on QuickBooks. Used to pay $250/mo for AnswerNet but cancelled because they emailed him message transcriptions 45 minutes after the call ended and he'd already lost the job. Now calls go to voicemail. He estimates he misses 10–15 calls a week. He's on his phone all day between jobs but can't physically answer while elbow-deep in a wax ring. He's never used an AI tool that talked to a customer and is suspicious of "AI receptionists" because his sister-in-law used one and it gave a quote that was 30% off real pricing. He'd pay $79–$129/mo for "something that just texts the caller back with my booking link in 5 seconds and lands them in an inbox I can answer from the truck."

Marcus is the buyer (one person). Marcus is the user (no team to train). Marcus is the budget owner (himself). Sales cycle: one phone call.

## Why this slice — and not the obvious larger segments

### The market reality

The research is unambiguous: missed calls are a massive, well-publicized problem in home services. Industry-average missed call rate is ~27%, and for SMBs it climbs to 62%. Average annual revenue loss per small contractor: **$45K–$120K**. 78% of callers buy from the first contractor who answers. 80% don't leave voicemail. 85% of voicemail leavers never call back. These are the numbers every competitor and customer already know — which means the problem isn't selling the problem, it's positioning your solution.

### The competitive landscape (denser than I implied earlier)

The space has stratified into three layers:

**Big platforms with MCTB as one feature:** HouseCall Pro, ServiceTitan, GoHighLevel ($97–$497/mo). Bundled into larger CRMs. Win when the buyer is already shopping for a CRM. Lose when the buyer just wants the text-back.

**AI receptionists for trades:** LeadTruffle ($229/mo), Intry ("AI dispatcher Emily"), Ignitvio, Allo, Rosie, Goodcall, Trillet ($49–$79/mo), NextPhone ($199/mo), VoiceCharm. Heavy 2025/2026 funding wave. Pitch: an AI has a back-and-forth conversation with the caller, gathers diagnostic info, hands you a "dispatch brief." Real strength, real risk (hallucinated pricing, weird tone, AI confidence).

**Purpose-built MCTB:** MissedCall Plumber, Podium ($289+/mo), and a long tail. Some are good, most are GoHighLevel resellers with a plumbing skin.

**Where Relay NW fits — and where it doesn't.** It's not an AI receptionist (you don't have an AI conversation flow), it's not a full CRM, and it's not a Podium-style multi-product suite. It's the simplest, fastest, cheapest credible option that does the one thing well — and that's actually a defensible position, because the AI-receptionist wave has pulled the competitive middle upmarket and abandoned the price-sensitive solo operator. The opportunity is to be **the anti-AI, anti-bloat, anti-$229-bill text-back tool for the 1-truck operator.** Position it that way explicitly: *"You text your own customers back. The owner shows up in their phone, not an AI."*

### Why owner-operators specifically (and not 3–10 truck shops)

Three-to-ten-truck shops are the LeadTruffle and Intry target. They have a dispatcher, they want CRM integration, they want SMS analytics, they will pay $229/mo. You will lose head-to-head on features. The smallest segment — one to two trucks, no dispatcher, owner answers everything — is where:

- The buyer's pain is most acute (he personally loses every missed call)
- The buyer's decision is fastest (no committee)
- Your simplicity is a feature, not a liability (he can't operate complexity anyway)
- Your price ceiling is real but reachable ($79–$129/mo replaces a $250 answering service)
- The big platforms structurally don't want him (LTV too low to support sales motion)

This segment is also massive. Industry estimates put solo-to-two-truck operators at 60–70% of US home-services businesses by count — but a tiny fraction of category SaaS revenue, because nobody serves them well. That's the gap.

## The trade-by-trade ranking

Within this slice, not all verticals are equal. Ranked by likely fit for Relay NW today:

**Tier 1 — start here:**

| Vertical | Why it's sharp |
|---|---|
| **Independent plumbers (1–2 trucks)** | Highest miss-pain ($1.2K+ avg ticket on emergencies), tech-friendly, already paying for software, easy to find via supply houses (Ferguson, Hajoca) and PHCC chapters. Crowded competitor space but the small-end is underserved. |
| **Garage door (independent)** | $200–$800 ticket, emergency-leaning ("garage stuck"), 80% solo/2-truck. Far less competitor density than plumbing. Many use FieldEdge or nothing. |
| **Junk removal** | High emotional urgency (move-out day, in-laws coming), $200–$800 ticket, growing solo operator segment fueled by 1-800-Got-Junk's franchise model creating independent competitors. Underserved by software. |
| **Mobile mechanics** | Fastest-growing trade segment, owner-operator, can't physically answer while under a hood. Lower ticket ($150–$500) but higher call volume per day. Almost no purpose-built tools. |

**Tier 2 — viable, secondary:**

HVAC service (1–3 trucks), tree service, septic, mobile RV repair, mobile detailing, pressure washing.

**Tier 3 — skip:**

Solo electricians (most leads scheduled, lower miss pain), painters and handymen (longer sales cycle, missed call rarely the difference), cleaning services (low urgency), pest control (low ticket), locksmiths (A2P registration is a nightmare for this category — lots of fraud), notaries (low LTV).

**Tier-zero anti-ICPs (don't sell here):**

Anyone with a receptionist or office manager. Anyone using ServiceTitan or large CRMs (already covered). Anyone whose leads come primarily from Angi/Thumbtack (their inboxes already handle it). Doctors, lawyers, anyone HIPAA or attorney-advertising regulated. Anyone outside the US (A2P 10DLC is US-specific). National franchises (decisions don't happen at the operator level).

## What they currently buy (your replacement targets)

This matters because budget reallocation is the easiest sale — you're not creating a new line item, you're swapping one.

1. **Live answering services** ($200–$400/mo for budget, $400–$1,800/mo for full): the prime target. The pitch is "your caller gets a text in 5 seconds vs. an email 30 minutes later, and you pay 60% less."
2. **Voicemail** ($0): harder sale because there's no line item to replace, but easier persona (cheapskate but pained). Pitch on the lost-revenue math, not the cost savings.
3. **Google Voice / personal cell** ($0): same as voicemail. Easy product fit, requires teaching.
4. **AI receptionists** ($49–$229/mo): a small slice of customers who tried AI, got burned by a bad quote or weird tone, and want a human-in-the-loop alternative. Highly qualified leads if you can find them.
5. **HouseCall Pro / Jobber's bundled messaging** (part of $69–$249/mo CRM): hard sale because they're already paying. Wait until you have integrations.

## The geographic wedge

You're called "Relay NW" — lean into it. Start in **one Pacific Northwest metro** (Portland, Seattle, Spokane, Boise, or Vancouver WA), saturate it, and use the density for word-of-mouth. Why concentration matters:

- Trades referrals are hyperlocal. A plumber's strongest endorsement comes from another plumber he meets at the Ferguson counter or in his BNI chapter.
- You can do in-person sales. Visiting a supply house once a week to introduce yourself to the manager will eventually surface 20 plumbers who trust the manager's recommendation.
- Local case studies carry disproportionate weight: "Marcus Plumbing on the east side recovered $4,300 in missed calls last month" beats any national testimonial.
- Support is easier when customers are in your time zone and you can offer to drive over for stuck setups (for the first ten customers).

Once you have 10 paying customers in one metro and a referral flywheel, replicate the playbook in a second metro (Phoenix, Charlotte, Nashville, Raleigh are the highest-growth markets for trades; Pittsburgh, Cleveland, Cincinnati have the oldest housing stock and highest emergency-call density).

## Where to find them — the channels in priority order

1. **Plumbing supply houses (Ferguson, Hajoca, Winsupply).** The branch manager knows every plumber in a 30-mile radius. Bring donuts, ask for an intro. This is how every trades-targeted SaaS gets its first customers.
2. **BNI chapters and local trades guilds.** Most metros have 5–10 chapters with at least one plumber, garage-door guy, or junk hauler each. Show up as a guest, demo to that one person, get the referral.
3. **PHCC (Plumbing-Heating-Cooling Contractors) state association events.** Sponsorship is cheap for state-level events. Booth interactions convert better than cold outreach.
4. **Facebook groups for trade owners.** "Plumbing Business Mastermind," "Service Trades Roundtable," "Garage Door Geeks" — these have 1K–20K members, are very active, and have predictable patterns about who shares value vs. spams.
5. **Instagram for trades.** "PlumberTok" / "ContractorIG" is real. A genuinely useful 30-second clip ("how I caught $1,200 in missed calls last week") will outperform any ad.
6. **Reddit r/plumbing, r/HVAC, r/contractors.** Low signal commercially but warm audiences for honest founder presence.
7. **Cold outreach via Google Maps + LSA listings.** Every business with an LSA listing is by definition a buyer of lead-gen software and has a phone number visible. Build a Loom-per-prospect outreach: "I called your business at 2pm, got voicemail, here's what would have happened with Relay."
8. **Last resort: paid Google Ads on "plumber missed call software."** Crowded keyword, expensive, but converts cold traffic that's already in-market.

For your first 10 customers, focus on channels 1–3. They're slower but the customers you acquire are stickier and refer more.

## The first 10 customers playbook

**Weeks 1–2.** Pick one metro. Make a list of 50 plumbing/garage-door/junk-removal businesses with 1–2 employees in that metro (LinkedIn, Yelp, Google Maps filter for "small business"). Call ten of them at peak hours — note who picks up and who doesn't. The ones who don't are your prospects. Send a personalized Loom (you on screen + their Google Maps listing + a demo of Relay) within 4 hours of the missed call.

**Weeks 3–4.** Visit 3 supply houses, attend 1 BNI guest day, post 5 useful clips to LinkedIn/Instagram. Goal: 5 demos booked.

**Weeks 5–8.** Onboard customers 1–3 in person if they're local. Charge $79–$99/mo. Skip the contract; offer "first 30 days free if it doesn't recover at least your subscription cost." Every customer gets a weekly check-in for the first month.

**Weeks 9–12.** Use customer 1–3 testimonials in everything. Ask each for one warm referral. Demo cadence should hit 2/week. Start charging $129/mo for new customers — your reference customers stay at intro pricing.

By the end of week 12 you should have 5–10 paying customers, $400–$1,300 MRR, and your first repeat referrals.

## Adjacent ICPs to test after winning here

Don't think about these until you have 20 customers in the core slice. But for the eventual expansion map:

- **Owner-operator landscapers** with $300K+ revenue and seasonal phone spikes (March–May)
- **Pest control independents** (high volume of urgent calls)
- **Mobile RV repair** (small but high willingness-to-pay)
- **Personal trainers and small fitness studios** (different vertical entirely; missed call = missed client booking; can validate if home services tops out)

## Honest risks with this ICP

1. **Owner-operators churn.** A 1-truck plumber who has a bad month cancels everything. Your churn at this segment will be 5–8%/mo no matter how good the product is. Budget for it.
2. **Their willingness to pay is real but bounded.** $79–$129/mo is the band. $200+ is a different segment (small CRMs). Don't try to charge $199 here.
3. **They are not high LTV individually.** $99 × 24 months × 60% gross margin ≈ $1,400 LTV. CAC needs to stay under $300 or the unit economics don't work. This means almost all acquisition has to be word-of-mouth, supply-house, or owner-operator content — not paid ads.
4. **The competitive wave is moving toward AI conversation, not text-back.** Within 12–18 months, the question "why not an AI receptionist" will get harder to answer. Your two responses: "because customers don't trust AI to quote your work" and "because we cost $80 not $230." Both are real. But the AI wave is moving fast, and you need a clear position on it.
5. **Geographic concentration is a double-edged sword.** Saturating Portland is great until a competitor copies the playbook in Portland. Have a defensible position (depth in one metro, then breadth) before you announce strategy publicly.

## Bottom line

The biggest mistake you can make right now is to position Relay NW as "missed call text back for home services" — that's a feature, not a wedge, and the field is crowded. The defensible position is **"the simplest, cheapest, no-AI text-back tool for one-truck trades operators in the Pacific Northwest."** Win 10 Marcuses in Portland. Use them to win 30 more in the PNW. Use that to fund the multi-tenant rewrite and the eventual expansion to other metros and trades.

If you do this right, you're not competing with LeadTruffle and Intry. You're competing with voicemail. That's a much easier fight.
